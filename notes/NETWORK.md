# Network connections

Every network connection StarCom makes is listed here: to whom, when, why, and what is sent. If a connection is not on this page, StarCom does not make it.

**The rule behind all of it:** no StarCom user ever receives another user's IP address. Every conversation, including a two-person chat, goes through relays. What travels between people is encrypted end to end, and the relays cannot read it.

| Connection | To whom | When | Why | What is sent |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DNS lookup of the entry points (only if the entry points built into this copy are names, not numbers) | Your own DNS resolver (usually your internet provider or router) | When StarCom starts | To find the network's entry points (SPEC section 3, "DNS Seeding") | The entry-point names built into this copy of StarCom. Your resolver learns that you looked them up. |
| Relay key request | Your relay (an entry point) | Once, when StarCom first links to it | To learn the relay's public key, so the link to it can be encrypted | A 64-byte request holding nothing but "which key are you?". The relay answers with its public key. |
| Your link to your relay | Your relay | The whole time StarCom is open. A tiny "still here" packet every 25 seconds keeps your router's connection open. | Everything you do goes through this one encrypted link | An encrypted session (Noise IK, ChaCha20-Poly1305) proven with your identity key. Inside it: your signed @name record, @name lookups, and the encrypted packets of your conversations. |
| Your @name record | Star DNS (the StarCom network), through your relay | When StarCom starts, every 30 minutes, and when your relay changes | So friends can find you by your @name | A record signed with your key: your @name, your public key, your relay's address and key, and an expiry time. **It holds no IP address of yours.** The relay stores it in Star DNS on your behalf, so no Star DNS node ever sees your IP next to your @name. |
| @name lookups | Star DNS, through your relay | When you add a friend, or open a chat with one | To find which relay can reach your friend | The @name you are looking for (inside your encrypted link). Your relay searches Star DNS for you. You check the answer's signature yourself. |
| Conversations (chat and voice) | Your friend, through your relay and your friend's relay | While a chat or call is open | To carry messages and voice | Packets encrypted end to end between you and your friend (Noise IK, ChaCha20-Poly1305). Each relay passes them on without being able to read them. |
| Hub traffic | About 4 other members of each hub you are in (your mesh neighbours), the members in your voice channel, and whoever joins with your invite, each through the relays | While StarCom is open and you are in a hub | To pass hub messages, presence, voice, the hub's layout and keys, and joins | The same end-to-end sessions as conversations: one per pair of people, shared by a direct message and every hub the two of you are in. A session nothing uses closes after 5 minutes. Each mesh neighbour also gets a short version check (about 110 bytes) every 30 seconds, so missed updates heal. |
| Volunteer bridge links (only if you turn on "Help the network") | Every StarCom entry relay built into this copy | While StarCom is open with the setting on | To carry other people's hub and first-contact conversations between entry relays (SPEC section 5, "Volunteer Peer Relay") | An encrypted link (Noise IK) made with a random key that is new every time StarCom starts, never your identity key. Through it pass only other people's encrypted packets, which you cannot read, and a sealed note saying where each one goes, which you cannot read either. At most 2 Mbps of upload. An entry relay accepts one volunteer per IP address, so if it still lists your previous run (for up to about 75 seconds after it stopped) it refuses the new one, and StarCom asks again after 100 seconds. |
| Volunteer relay announcement (only if you run `starcom volunteer relay`) | Every StarCom entry relay built into this copy | While the relay runs: when it starts, then every 4 minutes | So other StarCom users can find your relay | Inside an encrypted link (Noise IK): the relay's own public key, which is separate from your identity key, and its port. The entry relay learns your IP address, then sends one packet to that address and port asking "which key are you?", and lists your relay only if the answer matches the key you announced. |
| Asking for volunteer relays | Your relay | Each time StarCom links to a relay | To learn which volunteer relays exist | A request with nothing in it. The answer holds up to 8 addresses and public keys of volunteer relays that were checked to be reachable. |
| Checking saved volunteer relays | The addresses saved in `relays.txt` (up to 2 are used) | When StarCom starts | To use volunteer relays instead of the built-in entry points | A 64-byte request "which key are you?" to each saved address until two answer. Entry points fill any gap. |

## Who learns what, plainly
- **Your friend never learns your IP address,** and you never learn theirs. Each of you sees only your own relay. Your friend learns your @name and your friend code (your public key), as they must in order to know it is you.
- **Your relay** necessarily sees your IP address: it has to know where to send your packets back. It also sees which key your conversations go to, when, and how much. It never sees the content. It keeps this only in memory while your link lasts, writes no logs, and never passes your address on to anyone.
- **A volunteer relay** is another StarCom user's computer. If you use one as your relay, that person's StarCom sees your IP address and which keys your conversations go to, and when and how much, just as an entry relay would. It never sees content. Anyone can look up the list of volunteer relays, so a volunteer's own IP address is public by their choice. The first time you start with an empty `relays.txt`, StarCom still uses the entry points once to get the list.
- **Your friend's relay** sees your relay's address, never yours.
- **Volunteer bridges** (people who turned on "Help the network") carry hub conversations and first-contact conversations between entry relays. A bridge sees only the entry relays' addresses and encrypted packets. **It never sees any user's IP address, key, @name or messages.** Only the entry relays learn a volunteer's IP address. Conversations between friends who have talked before do not use bridges, so calls stay fast. If no bridge is online, conversations go the usual way.
- **Honest limit:** while the network has one entry relay, a bridge hides nothing from that relay. It still sees which of its users talk to each other (never what they say). Bridges start to add privacy once there are two or more independent entry relays.
- **When you and your friend use the same relay** (today, while the network has one relay), that one relay sees both of your addresses. When the network has two or more entry relays, StarCom links to two of them and picks different relays for you and the other person, so that no single relay sees both ends.
- **Star DNS nodes** see the address of the relay that stores or looks up a record, never yours.
- **In a hub,** members know who wrote each message (messages are signed and names are shown), who is online, and who is in which voice channel. **They never learn anyone's IP address.** Relays and mesh neighbours cannot tell which member's connection a message started from: hub messages travel by Dandelion++ (see HUBS.md). Nobody outside a hub learns that it exists.
- **Any relay will tell anyone who asks which address it sees them at.** The request is 64 bytes and the answer 28, so it cannot be used to amplify traffic, and it reveals nothing the asker does not already have. Nothing in StarCom asks for it yet; it is groundwork for direct connections between friends (PEER-RELAYS.md).
- **Anyone watching your internet connection** sees encrypted packets going to your relay, and nothing else.

StarCom makes no other connections: no update checks, no telemetry, no analytics.
